\chapter{Evaluation}

\section{Evaluation Methodology}
This evaluation combines tactical depth using puzzles and playing elo using stockfish to provide a balanced assessment of each architecture. The benchmarking pipeline is automated via the project scripts so that training runs can be compared without manual intervention.

\textbf{Benchmarking Pipeline:} Models are evaluated through bot-vs-bot round-robins and fixed-depth Stockfish matches.
\textbf{Metrics Defined:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Estimated Elo:} Derived from match outcomes aggregated across repeated runs against Stockfish and baseline models.
    \item \textbf{Average Centipawn Loss (ACPL):} Evaluates the objective quality of individual moves compared to a high-depth Stockfish evaluation.
    \item \textbf{Win/Draw/Loss (WDL) Ratios:} Analyzes the playstyle stability and risk profile of each model.
    \item \textbf{Puzzle Solving Accuracy:} Computed on held-out positions from the puzzles dataset to measure tactical depth.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Experimental Scope:} Six architectures (ConvNet, ResNet, GAT, GCN, Piece Transformer, Square Transformer) were evaluated across multiple dataset scaling checkpoints (10M, 100M, 200M, 500M, and 1000M parameters).

\section{Comparative Architectural Analysis}
Synthesizing the results reveals distinct trade-offs between the evaluated backbones:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{CNNs (ResNet \& ConvNet):} Provide the strongest baseline performance and highest training efficiency. ResNet achieved the highest peak Elo (2498), proving to be robust and highly effective for this dataset scale.
    \item \textbf{Transformers (Square \& Piece):} Deliver strong global pattern recognition and flexible feature extraction. The Square Transformer scaled well (peaking around 2134 Elo), but these models come with heavier computational costs and moderate overhead due to attention operations.
    \item \textbf{GNNs (GAT \& GCN):} While structurally aligned with piece relationships, they severely underperformed in practice. They were limited by batching overheads, smaller effective batch sizes, and flatlined early in training.
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion \& Limitations}

\subsection{Elo Estimation and Playing Strength}

The Elo estimates derived from logistic regression over match outcomes against fixed-depth Stockfish show that ResNet achieved 2498 Elo at the 200M data scale - a level broadly comparable to a strong national master - while ConvNet achieved 2112 Elo at 500M positions and Square Transformer achieved 2134 Elo at 1000M. These are impressive results for a supervised trained model with no search.

Chessformer~\cite{chessformer}, which reported 2347 Elo using a transformer trained on Lichess/Stockfish annotations. Our ResNet model beats chessformer while using way fewer parameters, Chessformer uses a 240M parameter model with architecture-specific positional encoding tuning that we didn't replicate. The Google DeepMind searchless chess work~\cite{searchless_chess} reported similar findings: large-scale supervised training on engine-annotated positions is a viable path to competitive play without tree search.

That said, Elo estimates from self-play or fixed-opponent matches are noisy. The logistic curve assumes a stable, consistent opponent, but Stockfish at a fixed depth is not exactly the same difficulty for every position type. Opening-heavy games and endgame positions behave very differently under the same depth setting. So treat the Elo numbers as a useful relative ranking across architectures, not as absolute strength claims.

\subsection{Win/Draw/Loss Profiles}

ResNet tends to produce decisive outcomes - it either wins or loses, with a moderate draw rate. This aggressive risk profile is consistent with the observation that the strongest CNN configurations learned to convert material advantages efficiently, as shown by the smooth evaluation trajectories in Figure~\ref{fig:eval_cumulative_resnet_1000M_2300}. Square Transformer, on the other hand, shows higher variance: more decisive wins at scale, but also more losses, suggesting it occasionally commits to speculative lines it can't always follow through on.

GNNs produce a very different picture. GAT and GCN's WDL profiles are flat and loss-heavy across all data scales, these models genuinely failed to learn competitive play. The WDL data also shows that GNNs weren't playing conservatively for draws; they were losing outright.

\subsection{Comparison to Literature}

DeepChess~\cite{deep_chess_paper} achieved grandmaster-level play using a supervised pairwise ranking approach on human games, but relied on a dataset of 640K CCRL games and a very different architecture (Pos2Vec + comparison network). The key difference here is the training signal: Stockfish-annotated Lichess positions with PV-unrolled diversity, rather than human game outcomes. The tradeoff is that Stockfish evaluations are consistent and high-quality, but they encode one engine's tactical style.

Giraffe~\cite{deep_learning_game_ai} reached approximately International Master strength through TD-learning self-play with minimal hand-crafted features. The CNN and Transformer models exceed that level: project benefits from far more training data and cleaner annotations, even if self-play RL would likely push further.

The GNN results are the most interesting deviation from prior work. AlphaGateau~\cite{gnn_chess} (Rigaux and Kashima) demonstrated that a GAT-based architecture with GATEAU edge features significantly outperformed a ResNet-based AlphaZero on a 5$\times$5 chess variant in sample efficiency. That finding didn't transfer here. On full 8$\times$8 chess with standard supervised training, the GNN architectures flatlined. The difference may come down to three factors: AlphaGateau uses MCTS-guided self-play rather than pure supervised learning, it operates on a much simpler board variant, and the edge feature design for 5$\times$5 chess is considerably more tractable than for 8$\times$8.

\subsection{Limitations}

There are several real constraints worth being honest about. Compute was the primary bottleneck. Training was capped at a single NVIDIA GPU, which meant GNN batch sizes had to be halved (1024 vs 2048 for other architectures), and the total training epochs at large data scales were drastically reduced - one epoch over 1B positions is simply not comparable to 50 epochs over 10M. This almost certainly disadvantaged GNNs disproportionately, since they tend to require more gradient steps to converge due to the aggregation dynamics of message passing.

The Stockfish depth used for both labelling (depth 18 in the Lichess dataset) and benchmarking sets a ceiling on what the models can learn. The supervised labels encode Stockfish's particular style and tactical priorities. Evaluations against top-tier engines at depth 20+ were not feasible within the project timeline. The Elo estimates therefore reflect strength against reduced-strength Stockfish, not against full-strength opponents.

Future work could meaningfully address these gaps by incorporating MCTS-guided self-play - as AlphaZero~\cite{alphago_paper} and Leela Chess Zero demonstrate - to push beyond the ceiling imposed by supervised learning alone. Architectural improvements to GNN batching, potentially using heterogeneous graph representations or sparse attention over piece subsets, might also unlock the relational inductive bias that makes GNNs theoretically appealing for chess.
